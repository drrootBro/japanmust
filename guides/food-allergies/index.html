Food allergies in Japan: how to communicate them

JapanMust Guides · 5 min read

Short answer: show your phone — screenshot the phrase cards below and show them to staff, and say how severe it is. Japan takes allergies reasonably seriously — packaged food has clear allergen labeling by law — but restaurant awareness varies, and dashi (fish stock) is in everything. Here's how to stay safe.

Show-your-phone phrase cards

Screenshot these and show them to staff. Written Japanese beats spoken attempts for something this important.

私は重度のアレルギーがあります
Watashi wa juudo no arerugii ga arimasu — "I have a severe allergy"
これに___は入っていますか？
Kore ni ___ wa haitte imasu ka? — "Does this contain ___?"

Fill in the blank with:

  • 卵 tamago — egg · 乳製品 nyuuseihin — dairy
  • 小麦 komugi — wheat · そば soba — buckwheat
  • 落花生 rakkasei — peanuts · えび ebi — shrimp · かに kani — crab

Hidden allergens to know

  • Dashi — fish-based stock in miso soup, simmered dishes, egg dishes, even some "vegetable" dishes. Vegetarians and fish-allergic travelers: always ask.
  • Soba — buckwheat noodles are often cut with wheat, and the cooking water is shared.
  • Curry roux — Japanese curry blocks commonly contain wheat and sometimes shrimp/beef extract.
  • Gyoza/potstickers — usually pork + cabbage, but wrappers contain wheat.

Where it's easier

  • Department store food floors & chains — allergen charts are often available; ask for arerugen hyou （アレルゲン表）.
  • Conveyor-belt sushi — touch panels list allergens per plate.
  • Shabu-shabu / yakiniku — you cook it yourself, so you control what goes in.

Be direct about severity

Japanese politeness culture can soften your message — don't let it. Say 重度 (juudo — severe) and 命に関わります (inochi ni kakawarimasu — "it's life-threatening") if that's true. Staff would much rather turn you away than risk it.

Quick answers

How do I tell a restaurant in Japan about my food allergy?

Show your phone. Screenshot the phrase cards on this page and show them to staff — written Japanese beats spoken attempts for something this important. Start with “Watashi wa juudo no arerugii ga arimasu” (“I have a severe allergy”), then ask “Kore ni ___ wa haitte imasu ka?” (“Does this contain ___?”) with your allergen filled in.

What hidden allergens should I watch for in Japanese food?

Dashi — fish-based stock in miso soup, simmered dishes, egg dishes, even some “vegetable” dishes. Soba noodles are often cut with wheat, and the cooking water is shared. Japanese curry roux commonly contains wheat and sometimes shrimp or beef extract. Gyoza wrappers contain wheat.

Where is it easiest to eat in Japan with a food allergy?

Department store food floors and chains, where allergen charts are often available — ask for an arerugen hyou. Conveyor-belt sushi, where touch panels list allergens per plate. And shabu-shabu or yakiniku, where you cook it yourself and control what goes in.
