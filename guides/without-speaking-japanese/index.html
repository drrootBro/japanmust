Can you visit Japan without speaking Japanese? Yes — here's how

JapanMust Guides · 7 min read

Short answer: yes, you can travel all over Japan without speaking Japanese — because almost nothing you actually need to do requires a conversation. Ordering food, buying train tickets, finding a platform, paying a bill: these are transactions, not discussions. Each one has a workaround, and most of them involve pointing at something. This guide walks through the situations where the language gap actually bites, one at a time.

Ordering food when you can't read the menu

This is the big one, and it's easier than it looks. Chains and tourist-area restaurants often have English menus — ask, because they're frequently kept under the counter. Small local places usually don't, and those are often the ones worth eating at. The full breakdown is in our guide to English menus in Japan.

When there's no English menu, work down this list:

  • Point at the plastic food models in the window or the photos on the menu, and say "kore o kudasai" — "this one, please." Nobody has ever misunderstood this.
  • Ask what's good: "Osusume wa nan desu ka?" — "What do you recommend?" Staff light up at this question, and you usually get the best thing in the kitchen.
  • Learn the shape of a menu — a few recurring kanji cover most of what's on offer. Our guide to reading a Japanese menu covers the ones that matter.

Two formats confuse everyone the first time. Ramen shops make you buy a meal ticket from a machine before you sit down — see how to order ramen for the machine routine. Sushi splits into conveyor-belt places with touch panels (often with an English button) and counter shops where pointing at the fish is normal — see the sushi ordering guide. And when the meal's over, asking for the bill has its own small ritual; there's no tipping, which removes the most awkward calculation entirely.

Ticket machines and getting around

Trains are where visitors expect the language to hurt, and mostly it doesn't. Major stations signpost lines, platforms, and exits in English. Ticket machines almost always have an English button — look for 言語 or "Language" in a corner of the screen before you panic. An IC card (Suica, Pasmo, Icoca) removes most ticket decisions anyway: you tap in, tap out, and the fare works itself out.

What actually goes wrong is big interchange stations — Shinjuku, Tokyo, Umeda — where the problem is architecture, not Japanese. Screenshot your route, platform number included, before you go underground. Signal drops between stations, and a screenshot doesn't care.

On buses, the pattern is simpler than it feels: in most cities you board, take a numbered ticket or tap your card, and pay when you get off. If you're genuinely stuck, show the driver your destination on your phone. Drivers have seen this ten thousand times.

Asking for directions and help

Japanese people are famously willing to help and often less confident in English than you are in Japanese — which produces a specific dynamic: someone may walk you to the place rather than explain how to get there. Accept the escort. It's faster.

Make helping you easy:

  • Show, don't pronounce. A map pin, a photo of the building, or the address written in Japanese beats your best attempt at saying it.
  • Start with sumimasen — "excuse me." It opens every interaction politely and buys you goodwill before the pointing starts.
  • Use the professionals. Station staff, convenience store clerks, and hotel front desks field lost-tourist questions all day. Your hotel can also write down destinations in Japanese for you — ask before you head out.

Allergies and things you can't eat

This is the one situation where "point and smile" is not good enough. If you have an allergy, carry it in writing: a printed card in Japanese that names the allergen, shown before you order. Our food allergies in Japan guide covers the key phrases and what to watch for, and the printable allergy card lets you tick your allergens and print one card to hand to staff.

The reason the card matters: Japanese cooking hides allergens in places a menu photo won't warn you about. Dashi — fish stock — is in soups, sauces, and dishes that look entirely vegetable-based. Soy sauce contains wheat. A spoken phrase can get misheard; a card on the table doesn't.

When you have no WiFi

Every workaround above gets harder the moment your phone loses signal, and it will: basement restaurants, subway tunnels, mountain temples. The fix is preparation, not a better data plan. Download offline maps and a Japanese language pack before you fly, and screenshot anything you'll need underground — our offline survival kit is the full checklist.

If you want a connection most of the time, sort it before departure: the eSIM and pocket WiFi guide compares the options. For translation specifically, set up an offline translator at home — a translator that needs the internet is a translator that quits exactly when the menu gets difficult.

Emergencies

Two numbers cover Japan: 110 for police, 119 for an ambulance or fire. Operators may not speak English. If you can, get a Japanese person nearby — or your hotel front desk — to make the call with you, and show your hotel's address in Japanese so help can find you or you can explain where you're staying. Keep that address as a screenshot, along with a photo of your passport's ID page. In a real emergency, the language problem shrinks fast: injuries and locations communicate themselves, and hospitals in major cities deal with foreign patients regularly.

The 10 phrases that cover most situations

You don't need Japanese. You need these. Screenshot this section — each phrase is written the way you'd show it or say it.

すみません
Sumimasen — "Excuse me." Getting attention, apologizing, opening any request.
これをください
Kore o kudasai — "This one, please." Said while pointing. The single most useful sentence in Japan.
英語のメニューはありますか？
Eigo no menyuu wa arimasu ka? — "Do you have an English menu?"
おすすめは何ですか？
Osusume wa nan desu ka? — "What do you recommend?"
トイレはどこですか？
Toire wa doko desu ka? — "Where is the toilet?"
いくらですか？
Ikura desu ka? — "How much is it?"
カードは使えますか？
Kaado wa tsukaemasu ka? — "Can I pay by card?"
アレルギーがあります
Arerugii ga arimasu — "I have an allergy." Say it, then show your allergy card — don't rely on this sentence alone.
お会計をお願いします
Okaikei o onegai shimasu — "The bill, please."
ありがとうございます
Arigatou gozaimasu — "Thank you very much." You'll use it constantly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Do you need to speak Japanese to visit Japan?

A: No. Millions of visitors speak no Japanese at all. Signs in major stations and tourist areas are in English, and the situations that actually trip people up — menus, ticket machines, allergies — can all be handled by pointing, showing your phone, and a handful of phrases.

Q: Can you get by with only English in Japan?

A: In Tokyo, Kyoto, and Osaka, yes, mostly. English thins out in small towns and neighborhood restaurants, but it never becomes a hard wall: picture menus, plastic food models, and translation apps cover ordering, and station staff deal with lost tourists every day.

Q: How do you order food in Japan if you don't speak Japanese?

A: Point at the picture menu or the plastic food model and say 'Kore o kudasai' — 'this one, please.' At ramen shops you buy a ticket from a machine first; many machines have photos and an English button. If you're stuck, ask 'Osusume wa nan desu ka?' — 'What do you recommend?'

Q: What if I have a food allergy and don't speak Japanese?

A: Don't rely on gestures for allergies. Carry a printed allergy card in Japanese that names your allergen, and show it before you order. Dashi (fish stock) and soy sauce hide in dishes that look safe, so the card matters more than any single phrase.

Q: Can I use trains in Japan without speaking Japanese?

A: Yes. Major stations signpost lines, platforms, and exits in English, and fare gates just need a tapped IC card or ticket. The hard part isn't language — it's big interchange stations. Screenshot your route before you go underground, where signal drops.

Q: What should I do in an emergency in Japan if I don't speak Japanese?

A: Call 110 for police or 119 for an ambulance or fire. Operators may not speak English, so if you can, ask a nearby Japanese person or your hotel front desk to call for you, and show your hotel's address in Japanese. Keep that address as a screenshot on your phone.
